Some time ago, I read a quantum error correction paper that really caught my attention. It focused on a specific adaptive syndrome measurement protocol, but the broader takeaway was what got me excited: classical feedforward can be used to drastically reduce logical errors.

It also forced me to rethink a lot of what I knew about decoding. Specifically, decoding hypergraphs (which are used alongside the syndrome to accurately correct errors) could no longer be built ahead of time. They had to be assembled just-in-time. Otherwise, memory requirements scale exponentially, making static setups completely impractical for most real-world systems.

Of course, many of us rely on Stim to generate detector error models and compile decoding hypergraphs. But its execution latency is simply too high for online, just-in-time generation, even on “slow-clock” platforms like neutral atom and trapped ion systems.

In our recent paper, we propose a parallel formulation of Stim’s backtracking algorithm and implement it in a new GPU-accelerated compiler called GreenPeas. On select benchmarks, it achieves a geometric mean speedup of 13.2× over Stim, enabling adaptive circuit simulations and deployments that were previously impractical.

Example: we circuit-level decoded [[4, 2, 2]]-concatenated surface codes with adaptive syndrome measurements by building decoding hypergraphs on-the-fly with GreenPeas. The result: 6.7× fewer logical errors and 4.5× faster decoding relative to prior phenomenological approaches.

GreenPeas is fully open-source. If you know C (and some of the good parts of C++), please consider contributing.

In general, a qubit can only encode one bit of information (due to Holevo’s Theorem). So, if Alice wants to send 2 classical bits of information to Bob using qubits, she must send 2 of them. But let’s consider superdense coding for a moment. Superdense coding is a protocol in quantum information that allows Alice to send 2 classical bits by sending one qubit, provided that they both already share an EPR pair (i.e. a maximally entangled pair of qubits). I’ll link a more complete explanation of superdense coding in the comments.

So, one way to think about superdense coding is the following:

  1. Alice prepares an EPR pair.
  2. Alice sends half the EPR pair (1 qubit) to Bob.
  3. Alice encodes the 2 bits in her qubit by applying some operation on it.
  4. Alice sends the qubit to Bob.
  5. Bob decodes the “new” qubit along with the “old” one and extracts 2 bits.

This seems pretty straightforward, and makes intuitive sense: Alice effectively sends 2 qubits to Bob, so in terms of capacity, it is no wonder she can “exchange” these 2 qubits with two bits.

But what happens if we look at the same protocol a bit differently? What if instead of Alice preparing the initial EPR pair, Bob produces it, and then sends one qubit to Alice. The protocol is still exactly the same, since our pre-condition is that Alice and Bob share an EPR pair. However, this time, Alice only ever sends one qubit to Bob, but still manages to transmit 2 bits of information!

Of course, this doesn’t violate Holevo’s Theorem - since Alice and Bob do share an EPR pair to begin with, which assists in increasing the classical capacity.

This perspective was first brought to my attention by my supervisor, Michael Ben-Or, and it is one of those cool examples where entanglement kind of “breaks” the classical intuition. I am thrilled to announce that our new paper, “Experimental Quantum Electronic Voting,” has just been published in Physical Review Letters!

Electronic voting is critical for democratic societies, but as computational power continues to evolve, classical voting schemes may become increasingly vulnerable to information leakage. Even real-world deployments—such as the system used in the 2022 French legislative elections—have later revealed critical security vulnerabilities upon reverse engineering.

To address these foundational limitations, our team has experimentally demonstrated an information-theoretically-secure and efficient electronic voting protocol. Crucially, our protocol leverages the unique properties of quantum states to operate without relying on election authorities.

Key Highlights from Our Work:

We based our experiment on a high-performance source of Greenberger-Horne-Zeilinger (GHZ) states operating at telecom wavelengths, making it compatible with existing communication infrastructure.

We realized a proof-of-principle implementation for a configuration with four voters and two candidates, strictly employing privacy enhancement techniques.

We also successfully demonstrated an election scenario supporting up to eight voters and 16 candidates.

Our protocol is particularly well suited for secure board-level elections within organizations or small-scale governmental contexts.

This work represents a significant step toward the practical deployment of quantum-secure democratic election procedures.
